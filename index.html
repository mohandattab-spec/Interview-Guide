Topic 01 · Dimensioning & Tolerancing
GD&T — Geometric Dimensioning & Tolerancing
📐 Overview
What is GD&T?
GD&T is a standardised symbolic language on engineering drawings that precisely communicates allowable variation in form, size, orientation, and location of part features. Governed by ASME Y14.5 (USA) and ISO 1101 (International). Unlike ± coordinate tolerancing which creates a square zone, GD&T uses geometric zones that reflect functional intent — providing up to 57% more usable tolerance area for position vs a square ± zone. It eliminates measurement ambiguity by defining exact datum references.

The 5 GD&T Control Categories
Form · Profile · Orientation · Location · Runout

Form controls — Straightness (⎯), Flatness (⏥), Circularity (○), Cylindricity — define the shape of a single feature independently with no datum needed. Flatness controls all surface points within two parallel planes. Circularity controls each circular cross-section roundness. Cylindricity simultaneously controls circularity, straightness, and taper of a full cylinder.

Orientation controls — Parallelism (∥), Perpendicularity (⊥), Angularity (∠) — relate a feature's angle to a datum. All three require at least one datum reference. Parallelism keeps a surface/axis parallel to a datum; Perpendicularity holds 90°; Angularity holds any specified angle.

Location controls — True Position (⊕), Concentricity (◎), Symmetry (=) — define where a feature is in space relative to datums. True Position is the most important, defining a cylindrical tolerance zone around the theoretically exact location.

Runout controls — Circular Runout (↗) and Total Runout (⇗) — measure surface variation relative to a datum axis during rotation. Circular Runout checks each cross-section independently; Total Runout controls the entire surface simultaneously and is the most stringent GD&T control.

Profile controls — Profile of a Line (⌒) and Profile of a Surface (⌓) — define allowable boundaries of complex curved shapes. Can reference a datum or work independently; ideal for aerodynamic or ergonomic surfaces.

🖊
Feature Control Frame & Material Modifiers
Reading GD&T callouts · MMC · LMC · RFS

The Feature Control Frame (FCF) is read left to right: [Geometric symbol] | [⌀ if cylindrical zone] [Tolerance value] [Modifier] | [Datum A] | [Datum B] | [Datum C]. Example: ⊕ | ⌀0.5 Ⓜ | A | B | C = "Position within ⌀0.5 at MMC, relative to datums A, B, C."

Material Condition Modifiers
  • Ⓜ MMC — Maximum Material Condition: Smallest hole / largest shaft. Enables bonus tolerance as feature departs from worst-case size. Used for assembly clearance/fit.
  • Ⓛ LMC — Least Material Condition: Largest hole / smallest shaft. Used when wall thickness or edge distance is the critical concern.
  • Ⓢ RFS — Regardless of Feature Size: Tolerance applies equally regardless of actual size — default if no modifier shown. Used for balance and runout.
💡 Bonus tolerance at MMC: If Ø10 hole has position tol 0.2 at MMC, and actual hole = Ø10.4, bonus = 0.4 → total tol = 0.6 mm.
📍
Datums & the 3-2-1 Locating Principle
Establishing exact measurement reference frames

A datum is a theoretically exact geometric reference (plane, axis, or point) from which measurements are made. Real datums are simulated by datum simulators — surface plate, precision mandrel, or chuck.

  • Primary datum (3 contact points): Largest functional surface. Removes 3 DOF: z-translation, x-rotation, y-rotation.
  • Secondary datum (2 contact points): Second surface. Removes 2 DOF: x-translation, z-rotation.
  • Tertiary datum (1 contact point): Removes final DOF: y-translation. Part is now fully constrained.
  • Selection rule: Always choose datums based on functional mating interfaces — not manufacturing convenience.
Interview Questions
Q1What is True Position and how do you calculate the actual deviation?+
True Position (⊕) defines a cylindrical tolerance zone centred on the theoretically exact feature location. The feature axis must fall within this zone.
Actual Deviation = 2 × √[(Δx)² + (Δy)²]
Example: Nominal X=50, Y=30. Measured X=50.2, Y=29.9. Deviation = 2 × √[0.04+0.01] = 0.447 mm. If tolerance = ⌀0.5, feature passes.
✅ Circular zone gives 57% more area than ±0.25/±0.25 square zone — a key GD&T advantage to always mention.

Sheet Metal Design
🔩 Overview
What is Sheet Metal Design?
Sheet metal design creates parts from flat metal sheets (0.5–20 mm thick) through cutting, bending, punching, and forming. A key task is developing the accurate flat pattern — the blank layout that when bent produces the correct final geometry. Critical parameters include K-factor, bend allowance, minimum bend radius, hole placement rules, springback, and relief cuts. Common materials: mild steel, stainless steel, aluminium, copper. CAD tools like SolidWorks and CATIA auto-compute flat patterns using the K-factor.

K-Factor & Bend Allowance
The mathematics behind accurate flat pattern development

When sheet metal is bent, the neutral axis is the theoretical line that neither stretches nor compresses. The K-factor is the ratio of the neutral axis distance from the inside surface to total thickness. It determines the arc length (Bend Allowance) consumed in the bend zone — directly affecting the correct blank length.

K = t_neutral / t_total Bend Allowance (BA) = (π/180) × Angle × (R_inside + K × t) Blank Length = L1 + L2 + BA
  • Soft copper/brass — K ≈ 0.35: Material compresses significantly on the inner bend surface.
  • Aluminium / mild steel 1018 — K ≈ 0.41: Most common design assumption for general sheet metal work.
  • Hard steel / stainless 304 — K ≈ 0.45–0.50: Less neutral axis shift due to material stiffness.
  • Wrong K-factor consequence: Bent parts come out over- or under-size — critical for precision assembly interfaces.
🔄
Springback
Elastic recovery after bending and compensation methods

Springback is the elastic partial unbending that occurs after bending force is removed. The material core retains elastic energy that causes the part to open toward its original shape. Springback increases with higher yield strength and lower elastic modulus (E). High-strength steel (AHSS/HSLA) shows 3–5× more springback than mild steel.

  • Over-bending: Bend to a smaller angle than required (e.g., 87° to achieve 90°). Most common method for press brake operations.
  • Bottoming: Punch contacts die floor — more plastic deformation, minimal springback. Requires 5–8× more press force than air bending.
  • Coining: Full-thickness plastic deformation at very high force (10–30×). Nearly zero springback but expensive tooling loads.
  • Stretch bending: Apply tensile stress during bending to shift neutral axis and minimise elastic recovery.

Interview Questions
Q1What is bend deduction and how does it differ from bend allowance?+
Bend Allowance (BA) is the actual arc length of material in the bend. Bend Deduction (BD) is the amount subtracted from the sum of both flange lengths to get the correct flat blank length — it accounts for the apparent shortening due to bending.
BD = 2 × OSSB − BA OSSB = tan(Angle/2) × (R + t) Blank Length = Flange1 + Flange2 − BD
💡 Most CAD systems use K-factor internally to compute BA automatically. BD is commonly used in manual press brake work and tooling sheets.

Sheet Metal Manufacturing Processes
Sheet metal manufacturing covers four operation families: Cutting (shearing, laser, waterjet, plasma), Forming (bending, deep drawing, roll forming, hydroforming), Joining (welding, riveting, clinching), and Finishing (powder coating, anodizing, deburring). Blanking, Piercing & Cutting Processes
Shearing, Laser, Waterjet, Plasma — differences and selection

Blanking — the punched-out piece is the product (blank); remaining strip is waste. Piercing — the strip with holes is the product; punched slug is waste. Die clearance: 5–10% of thickness per side. Too little → burr, punch wear. Too much → rollover, poor edge quality.

Laser cutting uses a focused CO₂ or fibre laser. Excellent accuracy (±0.05–0.1 mm), no tooling cost, complex profiles possible. Fibre lasers faster and more energy-efficient for thin metals.

Waterjet cutting uses high-pressure water (up to 6,000 bar) + abrasive garnet. No heat-affected zone (HAZ) — ideal for heat-sensitive materials, composites, thick plates.

Plasma cutting is faster and cheaper for thick steel (>6 mm) but leaves a large HAZ and lower precision (±0.5–1.5 mm). Only for conductive materials.

Deep Drawing
Forming cups and enclosures from flat blanks without cutting

Deep drawing pulls a flat blank into a die cavity using a punch. Material flows radially inward — no cutting. A blank holder prevents wrinkling in the flange.

Draw Ratio (DR) = Blank Diameter / Punch Diameter Limiting Draw Ratio (LDR) ≈ 1.8–2.3 for mild steel
  • Blank holder force: Must prevent flange wrinkling but allow material flow. Too high → tearing at punch radius.
  • Ironing: Deliberate wall thinning by reducing punch-die clearance (used for beverage cans).
  • Redrawing: For parts deeper than LDR, multiple draw passes with intermediate annealing are needed.
  • Earing: Non-uniform flange height from material anisotropy — minimised by blank shape optimisation.
⚡
Progressive Die & Compound Die
High-speed automated multi-operation stamping

A Progressive Die performs blanking, piercing, bending, and forming in sequential stations as the metal strip advances one pitch per stroke. Parts exit fully formed at 100–1,000+ strokes/min — no manual handling between operations.

A Compound Die performs simultaneous blanking and piercing in a single stroke — produces extremely flat, accurate blanks with tight hole-to-edge relationships.

  • Progressive die advantages: High throughput, consistent quality, ideal for terminals, brackets, clips, connectors.
  • Progressive die cost: Tooling $20,000–$500,000+. Economical above ~50,000–100,000 parts/year.
Interview Questions
Q1What is die clearance and what happens if it is too large or too small?+
Die clearance is the gap between punch and die wall per side, expressed as % of material thickness.
Correct clearance = 5–10% × material thickness t per side
Effects of Wrong Clearance
  • Too little (<5%): Excessive shear force, rough edges, punch wear, punch breakage risk
  • Too much (>15%): Large burr, rollover on cut edge, poor dimensional accuracy
  • Correct: Clean shear band, minimal burr, optimal tool life

Casting Parts Design
🔶 Overview
Designing for Castability
Good casting design minimises defects, reduces machining, enables clean mould release, and controls dimensional accuracy. The five key design principles are: draft angles for mould release, uniform wall thickness for even solidification, generous fillets to reduce stress concentration and improve flow, correct parting line placement, and avoiding undercuts where possible. Design rules vary by casting process — die casting needs less draft than sand casting due to its steel mould and ejector pins.

Draft Angle
Enabling clean mould release without tearing or sticking

Draft angle is intentional taper on all surfaces parallel to the pull direction. Without draft, the shrinking casting grips mould walls and cannot be extracted. Draft is measured from the direction of draw.

🔲
Wall Thickness, Undercuts, Parting Line & Fillets
Four interconnected casting design decisions

Uniform wall thickness is critical. Non-uniform walls cause differential solidification — thick sections cool slowly, thin sections fast. This causes shrinkage porosity (last area to solidify pulls metal inward), hot tearing (solidification cracking), and warpage. Keep wall variation within ±25% of nominal; use gradual tapers for unavoidable transitions.

Undercuts are features preventing straight mould extraction. Solutions: side-actions/slides (add $5k–$30k+ per action), lifters, collapsible cores, or redesign to eliminate. Always try to design undercuts out first.

Parting line placement affects accuracy, appearance, and tooling cost. Best practice: place at the largest cross-section, keep in a single plane, avoid critical sealing surfaces.

Fillets at internal corners prevent stress concentration, improve metal flow, and reduce hot tearing risk. Minimum R = 1–2× wall thickness. Sharp internal corners almost always crack in service.

Interview Questions
Q1What is Chvorinov's Rule and how is it applied?+
Solidification Time ∝ (Volume / Surface Area)²
A sphere solidifies slowest (highest V/SA); a thin plate solidifies fastest. Use it to size risers larger than the section they feed (riser must solidify last) and predict which sections are most prone to porosity. Shrinkage allowance: Al 1.3–1.6%, Steel 2.0%, Grey Iron 1.0%, Brass 1.5%.

Casting Manufacturing Processes
🔥 Overview
Casting Process Families
Casting pours molten metal into a mould, allows solidification, then extracts the solid part. The main processes are Sand Casting (expendable mould, all metals, low tooling cost, large parts), High Pressure Die Casting (HPDC) (permanent steel die, Al/Zn/Mg, high volume, net shape), Investment / Lost Wax Casting (ceramic shell, complex geometry, all alloys including SS and superalloys), and Centrifugal Casting (rotating mould, pipes and rings). Selection depends on volume, material, geometry complexity, and required accuracy.

Sand Casting — Step by Step
Most versatile process for all metals and large parts
  • Pattern making: Create pattern (wood/Al/steel) slightly oversize for shrinkage allowance. Add draft to all vertical faces.
  • Mould making: Pack green sand (silica + clay + water) around pattern in flask. Cope = top half, Drag = bottom half.
  • Core placement: Insert sand cores for internal cavities or holes.
  • Gating system: Sprue (vertical entry) → Runner (horizontal) → Gate (cavity entry) → Riser (feed metal). Remove pattern and assemble mould.
  • Pouring: Molten metal at correct temperature and controlled flow rate.
  • Cooling & shakeout: Allow full solidification. Break mould. Extract casting.
  • Fettling: Remove gates and risers, shot blast, grind, inspect. Tolerances: ±1–3 mm, Ra 6.3–25 µm.

Investment Casting (Lost Wax)
Complex geometry, any alloy, near-zero draft required

A wax pattern is created in a metal die, assembled into a tree, dipped in ceramic slurry 8–12 times to build a shell, then dewaxed in a steam autoclave. The ceramic shell is fired at 1,000°C and molten metal (any alloy — Ti, SS, Inconel) is poured into the hot shell. After cooling, ceramic is broken off. Because there is no parting line and no mould opening direction, almost any geometry including undercuts can be cast.

  • Tolerance: ±0.1–0.3 mm; Ra 1.6–3.2 µm — often used directly without machining
  • Best for: Turbine blades, surgical implants, jewellery, firearms components, complex aerospace brackets
  • Tooling cost: Medium ($5k–$50k for wax die); no expensive steel die as in HPDC

Plastic Part Design
🧩 Overview
Designing Injection Moulded Plastic Parts
Plastic part design requires understanding polymer behaviour — they shrink more than metals (0.5–3%), are weaker in the mould flow direction, and are susceptible to sink marks, warpage, weld lines, and residual stress if poorly designed. Key elements: uniform wall thickness (most critical), correct boss and rib geometry, functional snap fits and living hinges, adequate draft angles, and gate location for good mould fill. Guidelines vary by material — ABS, PC, PP, PA, and POM each have different optimal proportions.

Wall Thickness Guidelines by Material
Foundation of all plastic part design decisions

Wall thickness is the single most important variable. Thick walls increase cooling time (∝ thickness²), cause sink marks, and promote warpage. Thin walls risk short shots and structural failure.

🔘
Boss Design Rules
Mounting points for screws, inserts, and alignment pins
  • Outer diameter: 2.0–2.5× screw/insert outer diameter
  • Wall thickness: Maximum 60% of nominal wall — prevents sink marks on opposite cosmetic face
  • Base fillet radius: Minimum R = 0.25–0.50 mm to reduce stress concentration
  • Draft: 0.5°–1° on inner bore and outer wall
  • Height: Maximum 3× outer diameter without gussets. Taller bosses need ≥3 equally-spaced gussets (gusset t = 50% of boss wall)
  • Why 60% rule matters: Thicker boss wall creates a local thick section. Surface solidifies, thick core contracts and pulls surface inward — causing a visible sink mark on the opposite face.
🦴
Rib Design Rules
Adding structural stiffness without adding wall thickness
  • Rib thickness: 50–60% of the nominal wall it connects to
  • Rib height: Maximum 2.5–3× nominal wall thickness
  • Base fillet: R = minimum 0.5× rib thickness
  • Draft: 0.5°–1° per side on rib faces
  • Spacing: ≥ 2× rib thickness between adjacent ribs (airflow for cooling)
  • Orientation: Align ribs along primary load/stress direction for maximum stiffness contribution
🔗
Snap Fits & Living Hinges
Integral fastening and hinge features — design and material selection

Snap fits use elastic deformation to assemble parts without fasteners. Cantilever snap fits are most common. The arm deflects during assembly then springs back to lock.

Max Strain ε = 1.5 × δ × t / L² Allowable strains: ABS 2% | PC 4% | PP 6% | PA 4%

Increase L or decrease t to reduce strain. Engagement angle: 30°–45° for insertion. Retention angle: 90° = permanent; 15°–30° = releasable.

Living hinges are thin flexible sections (0.2–0.5 mm) integral to the part. Polypropylene (PP) is the only viable material for production living hinges — exceptional fatigue resistance, withstanding millions of flex cycles. Gate must flow across the hinge (perpendicular) to orient polymer chains along the flex direction. Flex while still warm after ejection (<2 min) to further align chains.

Interview Questions
Q1Why does rib thickness greater than 60% of wall cause sink marks?+
When the rib-wall junction is too thick, it becomes a local thick section. The cosmetic surface solidifies first. The thick junction beneath continues shrinking as it cools, pulling the already-solidified outer surface inward — creating a visible sink depression on the cosmetic face opposite the rib. Keeping rib thickness ≤60% of nominal wall ensures the rib base cools at a comparable rate to the wall, preventing differential shrinkage at that junction.

Injection Moulding Basics
💉 Overview
What is Injection Moulding?
Injection moulding is the dominant plastic manufacturing process. Granular thermoplastic is melted by a reciprocating screw, then injected under high pressure into a cooled steel mould. After solidification, the mould opens and ejector pins release the part. Cycle times: 10–120 seconds depending on wall thickness. Key parameters: clamp force, injection pressure, melt temperature, mould temperature, pack/hold pressure, and cooling time. Tooling cost is high ($10k–$500k+) but unit costs are very low at volume.

The 5-Phase Injection Moulding Cycle
From mould close to part ejection
  • Phase 1 — Mould Closing & Clamping: Moving platen closes mould. Clamp force built up to resist cavity pressure during injection. Formula: Clamp = Projected Area × Cavity Pressure × Safety Factor.
  • Phase 2 — Injection / Fill: Screw acts as plunger. Molten plastic injected through nozzle → sprue → runner → gate → cavity. Fill phase = 95–99% of cavity volume.
  • Phase 3 — Pack & Hold: Additional material forced in at lower pressure to compensate shrinkage. Held until gate freezes (solidifies). Critical for dimensional accuracy and surface quality.
  • Phase 4 — Cooling: Part solidifies in mould. Longest phase — 50–70% of cycle time. Cooling time ∝ (wall thickness)² / thermal diffusivity.
  • Phase 5 — Mould Open & Eject: Clamp opens, ejector pins push part out. Screw simultaneously rotates and retracts to plasticate next shot — parallel activity during cooling.
Cycle Time = Fill + Pack/Hold + Cooling + Open/Eject + Close (10–120 sec)

A cold runner solidifies each shot and is ejected as waste. A hot runner keeps plastic permanently molten using heated manifolds — no runner waste, faster cycle, better quality, but adds $15k–$100k+ to tooling cost.

Clamping Force Calculation
Sizing the injection moulding machine correctly
Clamp Force (tonnes) = Projected Area (cm²) × Cavity Pressure (MPa) × Safety Factor / 100 Typical cavity pressure: 20–50 MPa | SF: 1.1–1.25 Rule of thumb: 2–5 tonnes per cm² of projected area
  • Example: Area = 120 cm², P = 35 MPa, SF = 1.1 → Clamp = 120 × 35 × 1.1 / 100 = 46.2 tonnes
  • Undersize clamp → Flash: Mould opens slightly during injection, plastic escapes at parting line as thin fins
  • Machine selection: Choose machine with clamp 20–30% above calculated value to allow for process variation
Interview Questions
Q1What is pack/hold pressure and why is it critical to part quality?+
Pack pressure is applied immediately after fill to push additional material into the cavity to compensate for volumetric shrinkage during cooling. Hold pressure is maintained until the gate freezes off — after which no more material can enter. Too little pack/hold → excessive shrinkage, sink marks, dimensional undersize. Too much → overpacking, flash, internal stress, difficult ejection. Correct hold time is determined by a gate freeze study: weigh parts at increasing hold times until weight plateaus — that plateau point is gate freeze-off.

Plastic & Moulding Defects
🔍 Overview
Injection Moulding Defects — Three Root Cause Families
All injection moulding defects arise from three root cause areas: Part design issues (non-uniform walls, poor gate location, insufficient draft), Mould design issues (inadequate venting, poor cooling balance, wrong gate size), and Process parameter issues (incorrect temperatures, pressures, speeds, cycle times). The same visible defect can have multiple different root causes — always investigate root cause before applying a fix. The 8 major defects are: Sink Marks, Warpage, Short Shot, Flash, Weld Lines, Burn Marks, Jetting, and Delamination.

Deep Dive: Warpage
The most complex and costly moulding defect to resolve

Warpage is dimensional distortion after mould opening caused by differential shrinkage. Different areas of the part shrink by different amounts, creating internal bending stresses that deform the part after ejection.

  • Thermal differential: One mould half hotter → that side shrinks more → part bends toward hotter side.
  • Flow-induced anisotropy: Semi-crystalline polymers (PP, PA, POM) shrink more perpendicular to flow direction — creates bow in flat parts.
  • Glass-fibre reinforcement: Fibres align along flow, restricting shrinkage in that direction — extreme anisotropic warpage if flow is unbalanced.
  • Fix hierarchy (most to least effective): Design fix (uniform wall) → Mould fix (balanced cooling) → Material fix (glass fill) → Process fix (longer cooling). Address in this order.
Interview Questions
Q1What is the diesel effect in injection moulding and how is it prevented?+
The diesel effect occurs when air trapped at the last fill point is rapidly compressed by the incoming melt front. Like a diesel engine, adiabatic compression heats the trapped air to 400–500°C+ — charring or burning the plastic material. The result: black/brown burn marks at the end of fill (thin ribs, corners, last-fill extremities). Prevention: add vents (0.01–0.04 mm deep channels) at last-fill locations; reduce injection speed at end of stroke; reposition gate to avoid air traps; ensure mould vents are not blocked by material buildup.

Topic 09 · Dimensional Analysis
Tolerance Stackup Analysis
📊 Overview
What is Tolerance Stackup?
Tolerance stackup determines the cumulative variation of a critical assembly gap, clearance, or flush condition resulting from individual part tolerances. When parts are assembled, each part's dimensional variation combines — if total variation exceeds the functional requirement (minimum clearance goes negative = interference), the assembly fails. Two main methods: Worst Case (WC) — adds all tolerances directly, guarantees 100% assembly yield, conservative; RSS Statistical — uses square root of sum of squares, assumes normal distributions, guarantees ~99.73% yield at 3σ, less conservative but realistic for high-volume production.
Worst Case Formula
T = Σ |ti| (100% yield)
RSS Formula
T = √(Σ ti²) (~99.73%)
Software Tools
Excel, CETOL, 3DCS, VisVSA
Best Application
Assembly clearance & fit analysis
🔗
1D Stackup — Step-by-Step Method
The tolerance loop approach
  • Step 1 — Define requirement: What critical gap, clearance, or flush condition must be controlled?
  • Step 2 — Draw the loop: Trace a continuous path from one end to the other through each contributing dimension in the assembly.
  • Step 3 — Assign directions: Each dimension travelling in the positive direction gets (+); negative direction gets (−).
  • Step 4 — Sum nominals: Nominal Gap = Σ (nominal × direction)
  • Step 5 — Calculate total variation: WC: ±Σ|ti| | RSS: ±√(Σti²)
  • Step 6 — Check vs requirement: Nominal Gap − Total Tol ≥ minimum required clearance
Example: Housing 80±0.2 | Parts: A(30±0.1) + B(20±0.15) + C(15±0.1) Nominal Gap = 80−(30+20+15) = 15 mm WC Tol = ±(0.2+0.1+0.15+0.1) = ±0.55 mm → Min gap = 14.45 mm RSS Tol = ±√(0.04+0.01+0.023+0.01) = ±0.28 mm → Min gap = 14.72 mm

🛠
How to Reduce Stackup Without Tightening All Tolerances
Cost-effective approaches to improve assembly yield
  • Reduce contributor count: Eliminate parts from the tolerance loop through redesign — fewer interfaces = less stackup.
  • Tighten largest contributor only: The largest single tolerance dominates WC stackup. Tighten it for maximum benefit at minimum cost.
  • MMC bonus tolerance: For clearance fits, applying MMC in GD&T provides free bonus tolerance as features depart from worst-case size.
  • Selective assembly: Measure and sort components into bins — pair large-bin A with small-bin B to control assembly variation without changing tolerances.
  • Adjustment features: Shims, adjusters, or slotted holes absorb accumulated variation.
Interview Questions
Q1Why is RSS less conservative than Worst Case, and is it safe to use?+
RSS is less conservative because it assumes it is statistically unlikely for all tolerances to simultaneously be at their worst-case extremes — just as it is unlikely to flip heads 10 times in a row. For a normal distribution, the probability of a dimension being at its extreme is very low. With many contributors, the probability of ALL being simultaneously at worst case approaches zero. RSS at ±3σ gives ~99.73% yield — meaning 0.27% of assemblies (2,700 per million) may technically be out of spec. This is generally acceptable for non-safety applications at high volume. For safety-critical parts or low volumes (<100 units), Worst Case is required.

Interview Questions
Q1What is hydrogen embrittlement in electroplating and how do you prevent it?+
During electroplating, atomic hydrogen is generated at the cathode (part surface) and diffuses into the metal lattice. In high-strength steels (>1,000 MPa / ~32 HRC+), hydrogen trapped at grain boundaries causes a dramatic loss of ductility and toughness — the part becomes brittle and can fail at stresses far below yield strength, often hours to days after assembly (delayed failure). Prevention: bake parts at 190–220°C for 8–24 hours within 4 hours of plating to allow hydrogen to thermally diffuse out before it causes damage. This must be specified on the drawing as a requirement. For very high-strength steels (>1,400 MPa), consider alternative processes: mechanical plating, ion vapour deposition (IVD) aluminium, or physical vapour deposition (PVD).

Topic 13 · Risk Analysis
DFMEA
⚠️ Overview
Design Failure Mode & Effects Analysis
DFMEA is a systematic proactive risk tool used during design to identify potential failure modes, assess their risk priority, and implement preventive actions before product release. Governed by AIAG-VDA FMEA Handbook (2019) — mandatory for automotive APQP. Risk is quantified by RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection (each 1–10, max RPN = 1,000). High RPN items get action plans. Critically, any item with Severity ≥ 9 must be addressed regardless of RPN — because safety risks require mitigation even if occurrence and detection are favourable.

📋
DFMEA Column-by-Column Walkthrough
Complete 11-step process guide
  • 1. Item & Function: Component and its required function. E.g. "Boss — locate and secure cover plate with M3 screw."
  • 2. Failure Mode: How the item fails to perform its function. E.g. "Boss fractures under assembly torque."
  • 3. Effect of Failure: Customer consequence. E.g. "Cover detaches in use — safety hazard, warranty return."
  • 4. Severity (S 1–10): S=10: safety failure without warning; S=9: safety with warning; S=7–8: major function loss; S=1: no noticeable effect.
  • 5. Cause of Failure: Root cause mechanism. E.g. "Boss wall too thin — stress concentration at base exceeds material limit."
  • 6. Occurrence (O 1–10): O=10: almost certain; O=1: almost impossible. Based on historical data or engineering judgement.
  • 7. Prevention Controls: Current design actions preventing the cause — design guidelines applied, FEA performed, design rules used.
  • 8. Detection (D 1–10): Ability to detect failure before reaching customer. D=1: definitely detected before release; D=10: undetectable.
  • 9. RPN = S × O × D: Prioritise action list from highest RPN downward.
  • 10. Recommended Actions: Assign owner + due date. Target Occurrence first (design change prevents root cause), then Detection (add test). Never reduce Severity by ignoring it.
  • 11. Revised RPN: Recalculate after actions are completed — confirms improvement achieved.
⚠️ Severity ≥ 9 must ALWAYS be actioned regardless of RPN. S=9, O=1, D=1 = RPN 9 still requires a design change or protection measure.

Interview Questions
Q1Why is it wrong to only reduce Detection (D) when addressing a high RPN item?+
Reducing Detection means adding more inspection or testing to catch the failure before the customer — but the failure mode still exists in the product. You are now spending more resources to find and reject bad parts rather than preventing them. The correct priority is: first reduce Occurrence (change the design to eliminate the root cause — the failure no longer happens). Second priority: reduce Detection (add a test to catch it if it does happen). Only reduce Severity as a last resort (redesign to eliminate the hazardous effect entirely). Purely improving Detection without addressing Occurrence is not a robust solution — it is expensive, relies on inspection catching 100% of failures, and doesn't improve reliability in the field.

Effectivity, Disposition & Revision Control
When changes take effect and what happens to existing stock

Effectivity defines when the change becomes active in production:

  • Serial number effectivity: "Effective from Unit S/N 5000" — all units from this number onwards built to new design. Pre-SN5000 remain as-is.
  • Date effectivity: "Effective 2025-03-01" — all parts produced after this date must meet new spec.
  • Immediate: All WIP and new production implement now — typically for safety or quality stop-ship situations.

Disposition tells you what to do with existing stock and WIP:

  • Use As-Is: Existing stock at old revision acceptable — use until exhausted. No rework needed.
  • Rework: Modify existing parts to new specification. Rework traveller/instruction required.
  • Return to Supplier: Vendor replaces at their cost.
  • Scrap: Existing stock cannot be used — write off. MRB (Material Review Board) approval required.

Topic 15 · Design Methodology
DFM / DFA / DFX
🛠 Overview
Design for X (DFX) — Concurrent Engineering
DFX is a family of concurrent engineering practices that optimise a design for specific downstream activities. Research by Boothroyd and Dewhurst showed 70–80% of manufacturing cost is committed at the design stage — making DFX the highest-leverage cost-reduction activity available to engineers. DFM (Design for Manufacturability) makes parts cheaper and easier to produce. DFA (Design for Assembly) reduces assembly time, cost, and error rate. Together they are called DFMA. Other variants address Reliability (DFR), Testability (DFT), Serviceability (DFS), Environment (DFE), and Six Sigma quality (DFSS).

⚙
DFM — Design for Manufacturability
6 key principles that reduce manufacturing cost
  • Minimise part count: Combine multiple parts into one wherever function allows. Each eliminated part saves material, assembly time, inspection, inventory, and supply chain management cost.
  • Standardise features: Use standard drill sizes (R10/R20 series), standard thread forms, standard gauge thicknesses — reduces tooling cost, setup time, and inspection equipment needs.
  • Design to process capability: Never specify tolerances tighter than the process reliably achieves. Check process Cp/Cpk — Cp ≥ 1.33 (±4σ) typically required for production. Tighter tolerances = exponential cost increase.
  • Avoid secondary operations: Design so no deburring, reaming, spot-facing, or hand finishing is required — each adds cost, time, and variation.
  • Near-net-shape: Minimise material removed by machining. Design cast/moulded features in preference to machined pockets. Deep pocketing wastes material and machine time.
  • Accessible features: All machined features reachable with standard tooling. Drill depth ≤ 3× diameter (deep holes need pecking cycles, high tool breakage risk). No blind-corner machining.
🔧
DFA — Design for Assembly (Boothroyd-Dewhurst Method)
Reduce assembly time, cost, and errors
DFA Efficiency = (Theoretical Minimum Part Count × 3 sec) / Actual Assembly Time × 100%

The Boothroyd-Dewhurst method assigns a time penalty to each handling and insertion operation. The DFA Efficiency Index drives redesign to reduce part count and simplify insertion.

  • Minimum part count test: For each part ask: (1) Does it move relative to other assembled parts? (2) Must it be a different material? (3) Must it be separate for access/service? If all NO → combine with adjacent part.
  • One-direction assembly (Z-axis top-down): All parts inserted from the same direction — eliminates fixture re-orientation, enables automation.
  • Self-locating / self-fastening: Snap fits, press fits, bayonet locks — integral features eliminate separate fasteners and reduce assembly steps.
  • Error-proofing (Poka-yoke): Asymmetric geometry prevents wrong orientation — a D-shaft prevents rotational misassembly; a keyed connector prevents reverse insertion.
  • Chamfers and lead-ins: Every insertion feature should have a chamfer, radius, or taper to guide the part to position without forcing.
  • Easy handling: Parts should not tangle, nest, or require two-handed manipulation. Avoid flexible/limp parts (cable routing).

Design for Additive Manufacturing (DfAM)
Rules specific to layer-by-layer construction
  • 45° overhang rule: Surfaces overhanging more than 45° from vertical require support structures. Design self-supporting geometry wherever possible to reduce support removal effort and surface damage.
  • Z-axis is weakest: Tensile strength perpendicular to layers is 20–50% lower than in-plane for FDM. Orient critical load paths parallel to the build plane.
  • Horizontal holes print oval: Due to layer stacking, horizontal bores print slightly undersized and oval. For precision bores, print undersized and drill/ream to final size.
  • Minimum walls: FDM: 1.5 mm; SLA: 0.5 mm; SLS: 0.7 mm; DMLS: 0.4 mm. Below minimum = incomplete fill or collapse.
  • Topology optimisation: Use AM freedom to remove low-stress material — create organic load-following shapes impossible with machining. Tools: Altair OptiStruct, nTopology, Fusion 360 Generative Design.
  • Part consolidation: AM's biggest DFX advantage — merge multi-part assemblies into a single printed component, eliminating assembly, fasteners, and tolerance chains.
  • Conformal cooling (DMLS): Print mould inserts with cooling channels that follow the cavity surface — dramatically improves injection moulding cycle time and part quality vs straight-drilled channels.

FEA Basics
💻 Overview
Finite Element Analysis — What It Is & Why It Matters
FEA divides a complex structure into thousands of small, simple elements (tetrahedra, hexahedra, shells, beams). The behaviour of each element is described by simple mathematical functions; the entire structure is assembled by connecting elements at shared nodes. FEA solves [K]{u} = {F} — where [K] is the global stiffness matrix, {u} is nodal displacements, and {F} is applied force. From displacements, strains and stresses are derived. Commercial codes: ANSYS, Abaqus, MSC Nastran, COMSOL, Altair OptiStruct. FEA does not replace hand calculations — both must agree within ~20% or the model has an error.

FEA Workflow — 5 Steps
Pre-process → Boundary Conditions → Solve → Post-process → Validate
  • Step 1 — Pre-processing: Import and simplify CAD geometry (remove threads, small chamfers, fillets unless stress-critical). Assign material properties (E, ν, ρ, yield strength). Mesh: choose element type, set element size, refine at stress concentrations. Check mesh quality: aspect ratio <5, skewness <0.85.
  • Step 2 — Boundary Conditions & Loads: Apply fixtures (fixed supports, symmetry planes, rollers) to replicate real-world constraints. Apply loads (forces, pressures, moments, temperatures). Rule: always use the most realistic BCs. Over-constrained models underestimate stress; under-constrained models give rigid-body motion errors.
  • Step 3 — Solve: FEA assembles [K] from all element stiffness matrices and solves {u} = [K]⁻¹{F}. Linear static: fast. Nonlinear (large deformation, contact, plasticity): iterative solver, computationally intensive.
  • Step 4 — Post-processing: Extract von Mises stress (ductile metals), principal stresses (brittle materials), displacement, reaction forces. Identify hot spots. Calculate FoS = σ_yield / σ_von_Mises.
  • Step 5 — Validation: Compare to hand calculations (beam theory, Hertz contact). If FEA and hand calc disagree >20%, investigate — wrong BCs, mesh issue, or modelling error. Perform mesh convergence study.

von Mises Stress & Factor of Safety
Yield criteria and safe design thresholds

Von Mises stress reduces a complex 3D stress state to a single equivalent scalar for comparison with uniaxial tensile yield strength. Best yield criterion for isotropic ductile metals (steel, aluminium, titanium).

σ_VM = √[ ½ × ((σ₁−σ₂)² + (σ₂−σ₃)² + (σ₃−σ₁)²) ] Yields when σ_VM ≥ σ_yield Factor of Safety (FoS) = σ_yield / σ_VM_max

Interview Questions
Q1What is mesh convergence and why is it mandatory in any FEA study?+
Mesh convergence is the process of progressively refining the mesh and monitoring whether the result (typically peak von Mises stress or max displacement) stabilises to a fixed value. A coarse mesh is too stiff — it underestimates stress, giving a non-conservative result that could lead to undersized components failing in service. As mesh is refined, the solution approaches the mathematically exact answer. Convergence is declared when results change <5% between successive mesh refinements (e.g., halving element size). Without this study, there is no engineering basis for trusting the FEA results.
✅ Refine mesh locally at stress concentrations (fillets, holes) rather than globally — saves computation time while capturing accurate peak stresses where they matter most.
