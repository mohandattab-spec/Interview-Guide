Topic 01 · Dimensioning & Tolerancing
GD&T
Geometric Dimensioning & Tolerancing is a symbolic language on engineering drawings defining allowable variation in form, orientation, location, and runout of part features.
GD&T Symbol Categories
Feature Control Frame — How to Read
Q1What is GD&T and why is it used instead of coordinate tolerancing?+
GD&T is an engineering drawing language using symbols to communicate allowable variations of form, size, orientation, and location. Based on ASME Y14.5 (US) or ISO 1101 (International).
Key Advantages Over ± Tolerancing
  • More usable tolerance area: Circular zone gives 57% more area than a square ± zone
  • Function-based: Tolerances reflect how parts actually work
  • Bonus tolerance: MMC/LMC modifiers allow extra tolerance as features depart from critical size
  • Unambiguous: Eliminates inspector interpretation differences
  • Clear datum references: Establishes exact measurement references
💡 GD&T circular zone for position gives ~57% more usable tolerance area than a square ± zone — commonly tested insight!
Q2Explain MMC, LMC, and RFS. When do you apply each?+
ModifierMeaningUsed When
Ⓜ MMCSmallest hole / largest shaftAssembly clearance — bonus tolerance for fit
Ⓛ LMCLargest hole / smallest shaftEdge distance / wall thickness critical
Ⓢ RFSTolerance constant regardless of sizeBalance / runout critical (default)
✅ Bonus tolerance at MMC: If Ø10 hole with 0.2 tol at MMC, actual hole = Ø10.4 → bonus = 0.4 → total tol = 0.6
Q3What is a Datum? Explain the 3-2-1 locating principle.+
A datum is a theoretically exact geometric reference (plane, axis, or point) from which measurements are made.
3-2-1 Locating Principle
  • Primary datum (3 points): Largest flat surface — constrains 3 DOF (z-translation, x & y rotation)
  • Secondary datum (2 points): Next surface — constrains 2 DOF (x-translation, z-rotation)
  • Tertiary datum (1 point): Stops last DOF (y-translation)
  • Select datums based on functional requirements, not convenience
Q4What is True Position and how is it calculated?+
True Position (⊕) defines the exact theoretical location of a feature. The actual feature must fall within a cylindrical tolerance zone centered on the true position.
Actual Deviation = 2 × √[(Δx)² + (Δy)²]
Example: Hole at X=50.2, Y=29.9 (nominal X=50, Y=30)
Deviation = 2 × √[0.2² + 0.1²] = 2 × √0.05 = 0.447 mm
Q5Flatness vs Straightness vs Parallelism — what's the difference?+
ControlControlsDatum?
Flatness ⏥All points within two parallel planesNo
Straightness ⎯All points of a line/axis within zoneNo
Parallelism ∥Surface/axis parallel to a datumYes
💡 Flatness = self-contained. Parallelism = relative to datum. A parallel surface must be flat, but not vice versa.

Topic 02 · Design Guidelines
Sheet Metal Design
Sheet metal design involves creating parts from flat metal by applying rules for bending, cutting, and forming to ensure manufacturability.
Bend Allowance & K-Factor
Q1What is the K-factor in sheet metal and why is it important?+
The K-factor is the ratio of the neutral axis distance from the inside bend surface to the total material thickness.
K = t_neutral / t_total
K-Factor by Material
  • Soft copper, brass: K ≈ 0.35
  • Aluminum, mild steel: K ≈ 0.41
  • Hard steel, stainless: K ≈ 0.45–0.50
  • Wrong K-factor → blank over or under size
Q2What are the design rules for holes in sheet metal?+
ParameterRuleReason
Min hole diameter≥ material thickness tPrevent punch breakage
Hole to edge≥ 1.5t to 2tPrevent tearing
Hole to bend≥ 2t + bend radiusPrevent hole distortion
Hole to hole≥ 2× hole diameterPrevent web distortion
Q3What is springback and how do you compensate for it?+
Springback is elastic recovery (partial unbending) after bending force is removed.
Compensation Methods
  • Over-bending: Bend to smaller angle than required
  • Bottoming/Coining: Full pressure through thickness → nearly zero springback
  • Stretch bending: Apply tension during bending
  • High yield strength → more springback
⚠️ HSLA/AHSS steels have much greater springback — critical in automotive stamping.
Q4What is minimum bend radius? Typical values?+
Minimum bend radius is the smallest inside radius before cracking occurs on the outer surface.
Typical Min Bend Radii (× t)
  • Soft aluminum 5052-H32: 0t (bend flat)
  • Mild steel 1018: 0.5t – 1t
  • Stainless steel 304: 1t – 1.5t
  • Aluminum 6061-T6: 2t – 3t
  • High-strength steel: 3t – 5t

Topic 03 · Manufacturing
Sheet Metal Manufacturing
Sheet metal manufacturing covers cutting, forming, and joining operations to produce parts from flat metal blanks.
Q1What is the difference between Blanking and Piercing?+
OperationDesired PartWasteUse Case
BlankingPunched-out piece (blank)Remaining stripMaking flat blanks for forming
PiercingStrip with holePunched slugMaking holes/slots in a part
Die Clearance Rule
  • Clearance = 5–10% of material thickness per side
  • Too little → excessive force, rough edges
  • Too much → large burr, poor shear quality
Q2Compare Air Bending vs Bottoming vs Coining.+
MethodForceSpringbackAccuracy
Air BendingLowHigh — needs compensation±1–2°
Bottoming5–8× airMinimal±0.5°
Coining10–30× airNearly zero±0.1°
💡 Air bending is most common — less force, one tool set handles multiple angles.
Q3What is Deep Drawing? What is the Limiting Draw Ratio?+
Deep Drawing pulls a flat blank into a die cavity using a punch to form a cup/box. Material flows radially inward without cutting.
Draw Ratio (DR) = Blank Diameter / Punch Diameter | Max DR ≈ 1.8–2.3
Key Parameters
  • Blank holder force prevents wrinkling in flange
  • LDR = Limiting Draw Ratio — beyond this, part tears
  • Multiple draws with annealing for deep parts
Q4What is a Progressive Die and its advantages?+
A Progressive Die performs multiple operations (blanking, piercing, bending) in sequential stations as metal strip advances with each press stroke.
Advantages
  • High speed, high volume — parts per stroke
  • Reduced labor — automatic strip feeding
  • Consistent accuracy — all ops in one die set
  • Best for: small complex parts (terminals, brackets, clips)

Topic 04 · Design Guidelines
Casting Parts Design
Good casting design minimizes defects, reduces machining, and enables reliable production. Key considerations include draft angles, wall thickness, and parting line placement.
Q1Why is draft angle important? What values are used?+
Draft angle is taper added to vertical walls allowing the part to be pulled from the mold without sticking or damaging the mold.
ProcessExternalInternal
Die Casting (Al)0.5°–2°2°–3°
Sand Casting2°–5°3°–7°
Investment Casting0°–1°1°–2°
Injection Moulding1°–2°2°–5°
💡 Internal features always need more draft than external walls.
Q2What are undercuts and how do you handle them?+
An undercut is a feature that prevents the part from being pulled straight out of the mold.
Solutions
  • Side actions/slides: Move perpendicular to draw before ejection
  • Lifters: Angled pins that push part away from undercut
  • Redesign to eliminate: Best DFM solution
  • Sand cores: Breakaway cores for sand casting
⚠️ Every slide adds $5,000–$30,000+ tooling cost. Always try to design undercuts out first.
Q3Why must wall thickness be uniform in castings?+
Non-uniform walls cause differential solidification — thick sections cool slowly while thin sections cool fast, causing:
Problems & Guidelines
  • Shrinkage porosity — last to solidify pulls metal inward
  • Hot tearing — tensile stresses in constrained thin areas
  • Warpage — unequal thermal contraction
  • Al Die Casting: 1.5–5 mm | Sand Casting Steel: 5–25 mm
Draft AngleParting LineUndercutsWall ThicknessFillets
Topic 05 · Manufacturing Process
Casting Manufacturing Processes
Casting pours molten metal into a mold, allows solidification, then removes the solid part. Different methods suit different volumes, tolerances, and materials.
Q1Explain the Sand Casting process step by step.+
Sand Casting Steps
  • Step 1 — Pattern: Create pattern slightly larger than part (shrinkage allowance)
  • Step 2 — Mold: Pack green sand around pattern in flask (cope=top, drag=bottom)
  • Step 3 — Cores: Insert sand cores for internal cavities
  • Step 4 — Assembly: Remove pattern, add gating (sprue, runner, gate), close mold
  • Step 5 — Pour: Molten metal at correct temp and rate
  • Step 6 — Cool: Allow full solidification
  • Step 7 — Shakeout: Break mold, extract casting
  • Step 8 — Fettling: Remove gates/risers, shot blast, grind

Topic 06 · Design Guidelines
Plastic Design
Plastic part design requires understanding material behavior, moulding constraints, and structural requirements — bosses, ribs, snap fits, and living hinges.
Q1What are the design rules for bosses in plastic parts?+
Boss Design Rules
  • Outer diameter = 2× screw outer diameter
  • Wall thickness = 60% of nominal wall (avoid sink marks)
  • Fillet at base R ≥ 25% of boss wall
  • Draft angle: 0.5°–1° inner and outer
  • Height ≤ 3× outer diameter — taller needs gussets
  • Gusset thickness = 50% of boss wall
Q2What is a living hinge and how do you design one?+
A living hinge is a thin flexible integral hinge moulded with two rigid parts. Best material: Polypropylene (PP) due to excellent fatigue resistance.
Design Guidelines
  • Thickness: 0.2–0.5 mm (PP)
  • Width: 5–15 mm (wider = lower stress)
  • Parabolic profile distributes stress better than flat
  • Gate must flow across the hinge (perpendicular orientation)
  • Flex while still warm (<2 min after ejection) → millions of cycles life
Q3How do you design a cantilever snap fit?+
Max Strain ε = 1.5 × δ × t / L²
Design Rules
  • Allowable strain: ABS 2%, PP 6%, PC 4%, PA 4%
  • Increase L or decrease t to reduce strain
  • Engagement angle: 30°–45° | Retention angle: 90° (permanent) or 15°–30° (releasable)
  • Taper arm thickness for uniform strain distribution
Q4What are the recommended wall thicknesses for common plastics?+
MaterialNominal Wall (mm)
ABS1.5–4.5 mm
PC1.0–4.0 mm
PP0.8–3.8 mm
PA (Nylon)1.0–3.0 mm
POM (Acetal)0.8–3.0 mm
⚠️ Rule: Keep wall thickness uniform within ±25%. Use gradual 3× transition zones between thick and thin sections.

Topic 07 · Manufacturing Process
Basics of Injection Moulding
Injection moulding is the most common plastic manufacturing process — ideal for high-volume, complex parts with tight tolerances.
Q1Explain the injection moulding cycle step by step.+
Injection Moulding Cycle (5 Phases)
  • 1. Mold Closing & Clamping: Movable half closes, clamp force applied to prevent mold opening
  • 2. Injection (Fill): Screw injects molten plastic at high speed. Cavity fills 90–99%
  • 3. Packing & Holding: Extra material forced in to compensate shrinkage. Held until gate freezes
  • 4. Cooling: Part solidifies — longest phase, 50–70% of total cycle time
  • 5. Ejection: Mold opens, ejector pins push part out. Screw plasticates next shot
Cycle Time = Fill + Pack + Cooling + Open/Eject + Close = 10–120 sec typical
Q2What are the different gate types? How do you choose?+
Gate TypeBest ForLimitation
Sprue GateSingle cavity, thick partsLarge witness mark
Edge/Side GateMost common — flat partsEdge witness mark
Submarine/TunnelAuto-degate, multi-cavitySofter materials only
Hot Tip/Valve GateNo waste, clean markHigh tooling cost
Film/Fan GateLarge flat parts, min warpageManual trimming
Q3How do you calculate clamping force?+
Clamp Force (tons) = Projected Area (cm²) × Cavity Pressure (bar) × SF / 1000
Cavity pressure: 200–500 bar | Safety factor: 1.1–1.25

Example: Area=120 cm², P=350 bar, SF=1.1 → 46.2 tons
Rule of thumb: 2–5 tons per cm² projected area
✅ Undersized clamp → mold flashing. Always apply safety factor.

Plastic & Moulding Defects
Understanding moulding defects helps engineers design better parts, optimize process parameters, and reduce scrap costs.

Q2What causes sink marks and how are they prevented?+
Sink marks are surface depressions typically opposite a thick section or boss. The surface solidifies first; the thick core contracts and pulls the skin inward.
Prevention
  • Design: Reduce wall thickness, core out thick sections
  • Rib thickness = max 50–60% of nominal wall
  • Process: Increase pack pressure and pack time
  • Material: Semi-crystalline (PP, PA) sink more than amorphous (ABS, PC)
Q3What is warpage and how do you minimize it?+
Warpage is dimensional distortion caused by differential shrinkage — different areas shrink by different amounts creating internal stresses.
Design Countermeasures
  • Maintain uniform wall thickness (most important factor)
  • Symmetric part geometry for balanced cooling
  • Use glass-filled grades — GF reduces shrinkage differential
  • Gate into thickest section, flow toward thin sections
  • Balanced symmetric cooling channels — same temp both halves
Sink MarksWarpageFlashShort ShotWeld Lines
Topic 09 · Analysis
Tolerance Stackup Analysis
Tolerance stackup determines whether an assembly of parts with individual tolerances will function correctly at the extreme limits of variation.
1D Stackup Example
Q1What is tolerance stackup and why is it important?+
Tolerance stackup is the cumulative effect of individual part tolerances in an assembly. Each part has dimensional variation; assembled together, these add up and may exceed the functional requirement.
Why It Matters
  • Prevents assembly failures at tolerance extremes
  • Optimizes cost — loose where possible, tight only where critical
  • Identifies critical dimensions needing tighter control
  • Required for GD&T position calculations
  • Guides tolerance allocation across supply chain
Q2Worst Case vs RSS method — when to use each?+
MethodFormulaWhen to Use
Worst Case (WC)T = Σ|ti|Safety-critical, low volume, small n (≤3 parts)
RSS StatisticalT = √(Σti²)High volume, normal distributions, ≥4 parts
RSS with Cf=1.5T = 1.5×√(Σti²)Realistic statistical with guard band
💡 RSS gives smaller total tolerance because it's statistically unlikely all parts are at worst condition simultaneously. WC = 100% assembly; RSS ≈ 99.73%.

Topic 10 · Career
Projects — How to Present
Project discussions are critical in engineering interviews. Interviewers assess your design process, technical depth, problem-solving, and ownership.
Q1How do you structure a project explanation? (STAR method)+
STAR Method
  • S — Situation: Context, product, customer requirement, constraints (cost, weight, volume)
  • T — Task: Your specific responsibility and the design challenge
  • A — Action: Design decisions YOU made — tools used (CAD, FEA, DFM, DFMEA), iterations, trade-offs
  • R — Result: Quantified outcome — weight saved, cost reduced %, test pass, milestone hit
✅ Interviewers love numbers: "I reduced part count from 12 to 7, saving $0.45/unit × 500K = $225K/year"
Q2What key design decisions should you highlight?+
Highlight These Elements
  • Material selection rationale — why this vs alternatives
  • Manufacturing process choice — why injection moulding vs casting?
  • GD&T/tolerance decisions — what was critical and why
  • DFM/DFA improvements — how you simplified manufacture
  • DFMEA insights — what failure modes you anticipated
  • Trade-offs — what you compromised and why (shows maturity)
  • Validation — FEA, prototype testing, DVT strategy
Q3How do you handle a project that failed in an interview?+
Failures are valuable! Interviewers often rate honest failure discussions higher than perfect stories.
How to Frame It
  • State what happened clearly, no excuses
  • Explain what you learned technically (root cause)
  • Describe what you did differently as a result
  • Show the systemic change you implemented
  • Example: "Snap fit failed in drop test — I hadn't accounted for low-temperature ABS embrittlement. I added -20°C impact testing to our DV matrix and redesigned with a tapered arm."
STAR MethodQuantified ResultsTrade-offsValidation

Basic Materials
Material selection is a fundamental decision affecting cost, weight, manufacturability, performance, and service life.
Q1How do you select a material for a mechanical part?+
Material Selection Framework
  • 1. Define requirements: Mechanical, physical, chemical, manufacturing, cost
  • 2. Constraints: Min yield strength, max weight, temp range, regulatory (RoHS, FDA)
  • 3. Material indices: Stiff beam: E/ρ | Strong light beam: σy^(2/3)/ρ — plot Ashby charts
  • 4. Shortlist: 3–5 candidates passing constraints
  • 5. Decision matrix: Weight criteria, score each material
  • 6. Validate: Sample testing, supplier qualification

Topic 13 · Risk Analysis
DFMEA
DFMEA systematically identifies potential design failures, assesses their risk, and implements preventive actions before product release.
RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection
Q1DFMEA vs PFMEA — what's the difference?+
AspectDFMEAPFMEA
FocusProduct design failuresManufacturing process failures
OwnerDesign engineerManufacturing / process engineer
WhenConcept/design phaseProcess planning phase
ActionsDesign changes, material changes, test additionsProcess controls, poka-yoke, inspection
Q2Walk through the DFMEA columns step by step.+
DFMEA Columns (AIAG-VDA 2019)
  • 1. Item / Function: Component and what it must do
  • 2. Failure Mode: How it fails (e.g., "snap arm breaks")
  • 3. Effect of Failure: What happens to customer
  • 4. Severity (S): Rate 1–10 (10 = safety risk without warning)
  • 5. Cause of Failure: Root cause
  • 6. Occurrence (O): Rate likelihood 1–10
  • 7. Prevention Controls: Design rules, analyses, simulations
  • 8. Detection (D): Rate detectability 1–10
  • 9. RPN = S × O × D
  • 10. Actions + Owner + Due Date: Reduce highest RPNs
  • 11. Revised RPN: Verify improvement
⚠️ Severity ≥9 must ALWAYS be addressed regardless of RPN value!

Topic 15 · Design Methodology
DFM / DFA / DFX
Design for X (DFX) is a family of methodologies optimizing a product for manufacturing, assembly, reliability, sustainability, and cost.
Q1What is DFM? Give 5 key principles with examples.+
Top DFM Principles
  • 1. Reduce part count: Combine parts — fewer operations, tolerance chains
  • 2. Standardize features: Standard hole sizes, threads, gauges — reduces tooling cost
  • 3. Design to process capability: Tolerances achievable by process — check Cp/Cpk
  • 4. Avoid secondary operations: Design so no deburring/reaming needed
  • 5. Near-net-shape: Minimize material removal — reduce cycle time and waste
  • 6. Accessible features: Drill depth = max 3× diameter
Q2What is DFA? Explain the Boothroyd-Dewhurst method.+
Boothroyd-Dewhurst DFA Principles
  • Minimize part count: Does it move? Different material? Needs separate service? No to all → combine
  • Easy handling: Non-tangling, non-nesting, graspable from one direction
  • Easy insertion: Chamfers, leads, tapers guide to position. "One-hand assembly"
  • One-direction assembly: All parts inserted top-down
  • Self-locating / self-fastening: Snap fits, press fits eliminate fasteners
  • Error-proofing (Poka-yoke): Asymmetric features prevent wrong orientation
DFA Efficiency = Theoretical min parts / Actual parts × 100%

Topic 17 · Simulation & Analysis
FEA Basics
FEA divides a complex structure into small finite elements to predict stress, strain, displacement, thermal, and vibration behavior under applied loads.
Q1What is FEA and what fundamental equation does it solve?+
FEA divides a structure into a mesh of small elements. Within each element, displacement is approximated by polynomial functions, then assembled into a global system.
[K] {u} = {F} [K] = Stiffness Matrix | {u} = Nodal Displacements | {F} = Forces
Key Concepts
  • Node: Connection point with degrees of freedom (DOF)
  • Element: Small finite piece — tetra, hexa, shell, beam
  • Solving: {u} = [K]⁻¹{F} → displacements → strains → stresses
Q2What are element types and how do you choose?+
ElementShapeUsed For
Tetrahedral (Tet4, Tet10)4 or 10-node tetrahedronComplex 3D solids — auto-meshable
Hexahedral (Hex8, Hex20)8–20 node brickRegular geometry — more accurate
Shell (Quad4, Tri3)2D surface + thicknessThin structures — sheet metal, plastic shells
Beam (1D)Line + cross-sectionStructural frames, beams, trusses
✅ Mesh convergence: Refine until stress changes <5% between refinements = converged mesh.
Q3What is von Mises stress and Factor of Safety (FoS)?+
von Mises stress combines all stress components into one scalar value for comparison with yield strength (best for ductile metals).
σ_vM = √[½((σ₁-σ₂)² + (σ₂-σ₃)² + (σ₃-σ₁)²)] FoS = σ_yield / σ_max_vonMises
FoS Guidelines
  • Well-known loads, no impact: FoS = 1.25–1.5
  • Average design, moderate uncertainty: FoS = 2.0
  • Impact loads, human safety: FoS = 3.0–5.0
  • Fatigue: Use endurance limit vs alternating stress separately
